## X.1 Motivation

The experimental results of Chapter [5] establish the detection performance of the proposed framework under the standard single-dataset evaluation paradigm: training and testing on CICIoMT2024 at its published train/test boundary, following deduplication (Section [4.x]). However, single-dataset evaluation leaves open the question that dominates real-world applicability: to what extent does the learned model generalize beyond the distribution it was trained on? This question has particular force in intrusion detection, where prior work has documented severe performance degradation when models are transferred across datasets [Doménech et al.], and where evaluation protocols that permit information leakage between training and test partitions are known to inflate reported results.

This chapter therefore subjects the frozen detection core to three independent generalization measurements: (i) cross-dataset transfer to a different testbed (CICIoT2023), (ii) capture-session-disjoint evaluation within CICIoMT2024, and (iii) an operating-characteristic analysis of the entropy-based uncertainty gate introduced in Section [Phase 6C]. A fourth planned measurement — device-disjoint evaluation — was found to be infeasible on this dataset; the infeasibility analysis is itself reported as a finding (Section X.4.1). Throughout, we adopt the convention that a *decline* in performance under a stricter protocol is the expected and informative outcome: the purpose of these measurements is to characterize the model's behavior honestly, not to defend its in-distribution figures.

## X.2 Evaluation Design

Three principles govern all experiments in this chapter. First, *the frozen model is never tuned against the new evaluations*: where retraining is methodologically required (e.g., to accommodate a reduced shared feature space), the architecture and hyperparameters of the published configuration (Section [12.1]: XGBoost, 200 estimators, maximum depth 8, learning rate 0.1, subsample and column-subsample 0.8, softmax objective with inverse-frequency sample weighting; autoencoder topology 32–16–8–16–32) are reproduced without modification, and no threshold or parameter is selected using test-set feedback. Second, *deduplication precedes every split*, consistent with the dedup-first methodology of Section [Phase 3]; duplicate detection is computed over model-input features only, prior to partitioning, so that no duplicate pair can straddle a train/test boundary. Third, *preprocessing is fit on training data only*: all scalers and imputers are fit exclusively on the training partition and applied to test data by transformation alone. This constraint was additionally verified by code audit for the cross-dataset study, where its violation would silently invalidate the central claim.

Where the evaluated feature space differs from that of the published model (Sections X.3 and X.4), absolute figures are not comparable to those of Chapter [5]; in such cases the reported quantity is the *paired gap* between a matched baseline and the stricter protocol, computed under identical features, data, architecture, and seeds.

## X.3 Cross-Dataset Generalization (CICIoMT2024 → CICIoT2023)

### X.3.1 Protocol

CICIoT2023 [citation] was selected as the transfer target because it shares the CIC feature-extraction lineage with CICIoMT2024, maximizing feature compatibility while representing an entirely distinct testbed, device population, and attack campaign. Of the 44 features used by the published model, seven (including Srate, Drate, and the derived Magnitude, Radius, Covariance, and Weight statistics) are absent from CICIoT2023 and were dropped rather than reconstructed, yielding a 38-feature shared schema; the supervised classifier and autoencoder were retrained on CICIoMT2024 under this schema. The test set was a class-stratified sample of approximately 400,000 flows drawn across all 63 CICIoT2023 files, with rare classes retained in full; results are reported over three sampling seeds (mean ± σ).

Because the two datasets study different threat models — CICIoT2023 is dominated by flooding and Mirai botnet traffic, and contains web- and host-level attacks with no IoMT counterpart — two evaluation designs were used. Design A collapses both datasets to binary attack/benign labels, asking whether the fundamental decision boundary transfers. Design B evaluates only the five attack families present in both datasets (Benign, Spoofing, Reconnaissance, DoS, DDoS), with CICIoT2023 classes lacking a counterpart excluded and reported as out of scope. A naive 19-class evaluation was deliberately not performed, as it would conflate transfer failure with the datasets' differing attack coverage.

### X.3.2 Results

**Binary transfer fails at chance level.** Under Design A, the supervised classifier achieved a cross-dataset ROC-AUC of 0.6904 ± 0.0003 with balanced accuracy of 0.4998 and benign recall of 0.0005: the model classifies effectively all CICIoT2023 traffic as attack. We note explicitly that the corresponding F1 score on the attack class (0.964) is a base-rate artifact of the 93%-attack test composition and is not a meaningful headline; balanced accuracy and ROC-AUC are the honest metrics, and both indicate discrimination at or near chance.

**The one-class detector inverts.** The flow autoencoder, trained on CICIoMT2024 benign traffic, achieved a cross-dataset ROC-AUC of 0.2586 ± 0.0010 — significantly *below* random. Inspection shows the mechanism: CICIoT2023's benign traffic, originating from a foreign testbed, reconstructs with higher error than its attack traffic, which consists predominantly of homogeneous flood flows lying closer to the learned benign manifold. A code audit confirmed that no fitting operation touched the test data, ruling out leakage or mis-scaling; the inversion is a genuine distribution-shift phenomenon. To our knowledge, the observation that one-class anomaly detectors can *invert* — not merely degrade — under testbed shift, because "anomalous" is defined relative to a benign baseline that does not travel, has not been previously reported for this dataset pair.

**Family-level transfer collapses, consistent with prior work.** Under Design B, macro-F1 fell from 0.9037 in-dataset to 0.1965 ± 0.0002 cross-dataset, a 78% relative decline, corroborating and slightly exceeding the 66.87% degradation reported by [Doménech et al.] on the same dataset pair. Per-family F1 was: DDoS 0.39, Reconnaissance 0.34, Benign 0.20, Spoofing 0.04, DoS 0.01. A robustness variant merging DoS and DDoS into a single Flooding family — motivated in Section X.6.1 — recovered macro-F1 to 0.2578 (Flooding F1 0.4530), indicating that a real but minor portion of the degradation stems from the per-flow-indistinguishable DoS/DDoS boundary, while genuine non-transfer dominates. Reconnaissance transfers best among attack families, consistent with the intuition that scanning behavior manifests similarly across networks.

## X.4 Session-Disjoint Evaluation on CICIoMT2024

### X.4.1 Infeasibility of device-disjoint evaluation

The strongest within-dataset generalization protocol — holding out entire physical devices — was attempted first. Because the published feature CSVs discard device identity, features were re-extracted from the raw packet captures (72 flow-bearing files) with source and destination MAC addresses retained; MAC, rather than IP, serves as the identity anchor, since ARP spoofing forges IP addresses by construction. The resulting inventory shows that CICIoMT2024's testbed employed essentially a single attacker machine across attack scenarios [dataset paper §citation], with the consequence that only five devices qualify as class-specific "victim" devices across the 19 classes, and no class contains three or more. Victim-hold-out splitting is therefore statistically unsupportable, and forcing it would have produced a fragile, uninterpretable figure. We report this as a dataset limitation with a broader implication: published claims of device-level generalization on CICIoMT2024 that do not verify the device inventory assert a property the benchmark cannot support.

### X.4.2 Protocol

The evaluation pivoted to the strongest protocol the dataset does support: capture-session disjointness. CICIoMT2024's attack traffic is distributed across multiple independent recording sessions per class (e.g., four SYN-flood captures, eight UDP-flood captures); holding out whole sessions tests the model on recordings it has never observed. Features were the NFStream-native extraction (59 model-input features after excluding identity columns); following deduplication — which removed 37.92% of flows (Section X.6.2) — 4,567,803 flows remained, with all 19 classes retaining at least two sessions. Two size-matched arms were compared over five seeds: a *random pooled split* (all sessions merged, split at random — the leaky protocol) and a *session-disjoint split* (approximately 30% of sessions per class held out entire). As the feature space differs from the published model's, the reported quantity is the gap between arms.

### X.4.3 Results

The session-disjoint protocol reduced macro-F1 from 0.734 ± 0.010 (random pooled) to 0.475 ± 0.048, a gap of **0.259 ± 0.047** (35% relative), positive in all five seeds; MCC fell from 0.991 to 0.556. The flow autoencoder, by contrast, lost only 0.076 AUC (0.908 → 0.831): the unsupervised layer is approximately five times more robust to session shift than the supervised layer, providing direct measured support for the hybrid fusion architecture, in which each layer covers the other's characteristic failure mode.

Two further observations follow. First, a *protocol vindication*: for CICIoMT2024, the published train/test file boundary coincides with the session boundary — the test files are separate captures — so the evaluation protocol of Chapter [5] was capture-disjoint *by construction*, and its headline figures were earned under the honest regime. The random-pooling arm, which corresponds to the common literature practice of merging the published CSVs and re-splitting, inflates macro-F1 by approximately +0.26; this quantifies a leakage source affecting a substantial portion of published results on this benchmark. Second, the per-class gaps concentrate exactly where Section X.6.1's structural argument predicts: the largest declines occur in the TCP/UDP flooding classes (DoS-TCP gap 0.68, with near-total collapse in two of five seeds; DDoS-TCP 0.45; DoS-SYN 0.41), while the MQTT protocol attacks are nearly session-invariant (gaps 0.02–0.08) — protocol-level attack signatures genuinely travel.

## X.5 Operating Characteristic of the Entropy Gate

### X.5.1 Protocol

The entropy-based uncertainty mechanism of Section [Phase 6C] routes low-confidence predictions to human review rather than automatic decision. This section characterizes that mechanism as a full accuracy–coverage curve, computed post hoc over the frozen model's stored softmax outputs on the deduplicated published test set (892,268 flows; the stored predictions were verified to match the probability argmax exactly, and no inference or training was performed). Two thresholds were swept independently: a confidence floor τ_c over the maximum softmax probability (16 points) and a ceiling τ_H over normalized entropy H/ln 19 (30 points). We emphasize the epistemic status of this analysis: thresholds are *characterized* on the test set as an operating-characteristic measurement, in the manner of a ROC analysis; none was used for model selection.

### X.5.2 Results

At the thesis's operational threshold (normalized entropy 0.134, corresponding to the 95th percentile of benign validation entropy), the gate defers 1.676% of flows to review while retaining 98.32% coverage at 0.9986 accuracy — and the deferred slice contains **81.1% of all the model's errors**. The deferred set's would-be error rate (0.355) exceeds the overall rate (0.0073) by a factor of approximately 48: errors are heavily concentrated precisely in the flows the gate flags. Critically, the gate targets *incorrect predictions* rather than difficult classes wholesale: among the zero-day-prone classes of Section [Phase 6C], 69–90% of misclassifications are deferred while only 32–48% of each class's flows are (e.g., MQTT-Malformed: 90% of errors deferred at 32% class deferral).

The full curve provides deployment-relevant operating points: a lightweight floor (τ_c = 0.5) defers only 763 flows per million per day at 99.31% retained accuracy — a human-scale review queue — while the thesis threshold trades a 22-fold larger queue for 99.86%. Two honest caveats attach. The review queue is dominated by a single class (MQTT-DDoS-Publish-Flood, 37.6% of deferrals), indicating that per-class threshold recalibration is the natural queue-reduction path; and 18.9% of errors pass the gate *confidently wrong* — the structural failure mode that confidence-based deferral cannot capture, and the measured rationale for the anomaly-detection layers of the fusion architecture, which exist to catch precisely the inputs on which the supervised layer is confidently mistaken.

## X.6 Synthesis and Structural Findings

### X.6.1 Per-flow indistinguishability of DoS and DDoS

Both studies independently expose the same structural fact: a single network flow cannot, in principle, encode the number of attacking sources, which is the defining difference between DoS and DDoS. The near-perfect in-dataset separation of these families (Chapter [5]) is therefore attributable to session-specific artifacts — capture-particular rates and inter-arrival regimes — rather than to a learnable per-flow signature. This explanation is supported from two directions: session-disjoint evaluation collapses precisely these families (Section X.4.3), and cross-dataset confusion concentrates on the same boundary, with family merging recovering a measurable fraction of the loss (Section X.3.2). We conclude that per-flow DoS/DDoS discrimination claims on this benchmark should be interpreted as session-artifact learning.

### X.6.2 Duplication is a property of the traffic, not the extractor

The independent NFStream re-extraction of Section X.4 exhibited a duplicate rate of 37.92% (7.36M → 4.57M flows), closely matching the 37% found in the published CSVs (Section [Phase 3]) despite an entirely different feature-extraction pipeline. Sensitivity analysis shows the rate is robust to key definition (±0.56% including ports; cross-class duplicates 0.14%), and the reconnaissance classes reach 97–99.9% duplication, consistent with the near-identical nature of scan probes. Duplication in CICIoMT2024 therefore originates in the captured traffic itself, and deduplication-first evaluation is validated as extractor-independent.

### X.6.3 Overall picture

The supervised layer's precision decays monotonically with distance from the training distribution — fragile across sessions, at chance across testbeds — while the one-class layer survives session shift but inverts under full testbed shift. Neither layer alone survives deployment on an unseen network. These are the measured premises for the deployment posture proposed in Chapter [6]: per-site baseline learning during a shadow-mode onboarding period, human-in-the-loop review budgeted by the operating curve of Section X.5, and continual local adaptation — properties that the transfer measurements show are not optional refinements but conditions of applicability.

## X.7 Threats to Validity

The cross-dataset study evaluates a shared-feature retrain rather than the exact published model, as seven features are unavailable in the target dataset; the session-disjoint study likewise operates in the NFStream feature space, and both are therefore reported as paired gaps rather than absolute comparisons. The cross-dataset test set is a stratified sample (~400k flows) rather than the full 8.5 GB corpus, mitigated by per-seed stability (σ ≤ 0.001 on all headline figures). Small-support classes inflate individual per-class gaps (e.g., Recon-Ping-Sweep retains 113 flows post-deduplication). The τ-sweep characterizes thresholds post hoc on the test set and makes no model-selection claim. Finally, all measurements concern one dataset pair; the direction of the findings is corroborated by [Doménech et al.], but their magnitudes should not be assumed universal.
